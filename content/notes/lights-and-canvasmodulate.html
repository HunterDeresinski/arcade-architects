Difficulty: ⭐⭐ moderate · Payoff: ⭐⭐⭐ high · Time: 25 minutes

Darkness, and lights that cut through it. A torch in a cave, a flashlight beam, a spooky night level, a glowing pickup. Enormous mood change for about fifteen minutes of work.

#The two-node trick

Lighting in 2D Godot is basically one idea:

  1. CanvasModulate — tints the entire scene. Set it dark. Now it's night.
  2. PointLight2D — adds light back in specific places.
Main
├── CanvasModulate      Color: (0.15, 0.15, 0.3)   ← dark blue night
├── TileMapLayer
└── Player
    └── PointLight2D    ← a torch that follows you

That's it. Two nodes, and a bright level becomes a cave.

💡Never use pure black

Color(0, 0, 0) makes unlit areas totally invisible and unplayable. Dark blue (0.15, 0.15, 0.3) or dark purple reads as night while letting players still see shapes. Real night isn't black either.

#PointLight2D

It needs a texture. A PointLight2D with an empty Texture slot does nothing — this is the #1 mistake here, same shape as the empty-shape problem in CollisionShape2D.

Use a soft white radial gradient (a blurry white circle on transparent). Grab one from Asset Resources, or make one in seconds: add a GradientTexture2D, set Fill to Radial, and make the gradient run white → transparent.

PropertyDoes
Texturethe light's shape. Required
Texture Scalehow big the light is
Colorlight colour — warm orange for fire, pale blue for moonlight
Energybrightness. Above 1.0 blows out to white
Blend ModeAdd (normal light), Subtract (darkness), Mix
Shadow → Enabledlets objects cast shadows

A flickering torch is three lines (Tweens):

func _ready():
	var t = create_tween().set_loops()
	t.tween_property($Light, "energy", 0.85, 0.12)
	t.tween_property($Light, "energy", 1.05, 0.18)

Or add slight randomness per frame for a real fire feel:

func _process(_delta):
	$Light.energy = 0.9 + randf() * 0.2

#Shadows

  1. On the PointLight2D: Shadow → Enabled ✅.
  2. On anything that should block light: add a LightOccluder2D child.
  3. Give the occluder an Occluder Polygon and draw the outline.

For a TileMapLayer, you can set occlusion per-tile in the TileSet editor (Occlusion Layers) — paint your walls and shadows just work.

Shadows are expensive and fiddly. Skip them unless a team specifically wants a stealth or horror game. Lights without shadows already look great.

#A flashlight cone

Use a cone-shaped texture instead of a circle, and rotate the light to face the aim direction:

func _process(_delta):
	$Flashlight.rotation = (get_global_mouse_position() - global_position).angle()

With shadows enabled and a RayCast2D vision check on the enemies, that's a stealth game.

#Day/night cycle

@export var day_length = 60.0
var time = 0.0

func _process(delta):
	time = fmod(time + delta, day_length)
	var t = time / day_length
	var brightness = 0.25 + 0.75 * (0.5 + 0.5 * cos(t * TAU))
	$CanvasModulate.color = Color(brightness, brightness, brightness * 1.1)

#Making things glow

A UI or sprite can glow without any lighting setup:

  1. Add a WorldEnvironment node.
  2. Environment → New Environment → Glow → Enabled ✅.
  3. Set Glow HDR Threshold around 0.9.
  4. Anything brighter than that threshold blooms.

Then push a sprite's modulate above 1: $Coin.modulate = Color(2.0, 1.8, 0.5). It now glows. Great for Collectables, neon, and lasers.

⚠️Renderer matters

Glow needs Forward+ or Mobile, not Compatibility. Day 1 sets up Compatibility for school laptops, so glow may be unavailable. Lights and CanvasModulate work in all three — glow is the one to check.

#Where to use it

  • A cave or dungeon level — the classic
  • A spooky night level for a horror-ish game
  • A torch pickup that extends your light radius (a great mechanic — light as a resource)
  • Glowing coins, neon arcade aesthetics
  • A boss room that darkens when the fight starts
  • Lightning flashes: tween CanvasModulate to white for 0.05s
