🏠 Curriculum Map · Used on Day 3 - Design Day

Play your game on paper before you build it in Godot. It takes 20 minutes and saves you from building the wrong thing. This is not optional on Day 3.

#Why bother?

Code is slow to change; paper is instant. On paper you find out in 5 minutes whether your idea is fun or confusing — before you spend two days coding it. If it's boring as paper, it'll be boring as code.

#How to do it (15–20 min)

  1. Draw the screen. A big rectangle = your game screen. Sketch where the player starts and where things appear.
  2. Make the pieces. Tear paper scraps or use coins/erasers for the player, enemies, coins, goal.
  3. Be the computer. One teammate "runs" the game by moving the pieces. Another is the player and says their moves out loud: "I press right... now I jump."
  4. Play 30 seconds of it. Walk through a real round: starting, the core loop, and how it ends (Tiny Game Design).
  5. Hand it to another team. Let them "play" it. Watch where they get confused — that's a real design problem you just caught for free.

#What you're looking for

  • Is the core loop actually fun, or just an idea? (Tiny Game Design)
  • Is it clear what to do without explaining?
  • Is it too big? If your paper round takes 10 minutes, your game is too big (The Tiny Game Rules).

#Then write it down

Turn what worked into a short build list — that becomes your MVP Checklist on Day 4. List the pieces you need: player, one enemy, score, win/lose.

💡Don't fall in love with paper

The prototype is disposable. Its only job is to answer "is this fun and clear?" Once you know, recycle it and open Godot.
