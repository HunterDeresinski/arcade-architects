#Design Crash Course

Your instructor will walk through the three ingredients in every tiny game that works. Take notes — you'll need these today.

#The three ingredients

1. A clear player action. What is the one thing the player does? Dodge. Collect. Jump. Shoot. Avoid. Stack. If you need two sentences to describe what the player does, you have two games.

2. A tension. What makes the player care? A timer counting down, something chasing them, a score going up, a fragile thing they're protecting. Without tension, nothing matters.

3. A failure state. What ends the run? Getting hit, running out of time, dropping the thing, the meter filling up. The failure state turns the player action into a challenge.

💡The formula

Good tiny games are: [action] + [tension] + [failure state] Example: Dodge the Creeps = move + enemies keep coming + getting hit ends the run. Your turn: fill in the blanks for a game you love. Does it fit?

See Tiny Game Design for more examples and The Tiny Game Rules for the constraints your game must meet.

#Brainstorm with Constraint Cards

Work alone for this part — you'll form teams after break.

  1. Draw one constraint card from the deck on your instructor's table. Read Constraint Cards to understand what yours means. Tape it to the corner of your notebook.
  2. Your constraint is your creative limit. Use it. Set a 10-minute timer.
  3. In your notebook, write down at least 5 game ideas that fit your constraint. Don't judge them yet — just generate. Bad ideas are part of the process.
  4. When the timer ends, circle your top 2 ideas — the ones that excite you most.
  5. For each of your top 2, fill in the formula from the crash course:
    • Player action: ___
    • Tension: ___
    • Failure state: ___
  6. Pick your one favorite idea to pitch to teammates after break.

Your constraint (one of these — or draw your own):

  • One screen — the whole game fits on one screen with no scrolling
  • One mechanic — the player really only does one thing
  • One button — the entire game is played with a single key
💡Idea not coming? Try these prompts
  • "What if Dodge the Creeps but you're collecting instead of dodging?"
  • "What if the player is the enemy?"
  • "What if the goal is to make the level harder, not easier?"
  • "What emotion do you want the player to feel?"

#Form Teams + Pitch Approval

#Form teams (10:10–10:20)

Your instructor will help organize teams of 3–4 people. Here's how it works:

  1. Each person takes 30 seconds to pitch their top idea to the room (or to a small group).
  2. Your instructor will help you find the 3–4 ideas that are most buildable and most distinct from each other.
  3. You then join the team building the idea that excites you most.

Teams are formed around ideas, not just friend groups.

#Write your one-sentence pitch (10:20–10:35)

With your team, fill in the blank:

You are a ____ who ____ until ____.

Examples:

  • "You are a raccoon who stacks trash higher and higher until it topples."
  • "You are a single firefly who must light 10 lanterns before dawn."
  • "You are a blobfish who sinks deeper and deeper until you hit the floor."

Rules for a good pitch:

  • One sentence only. No "and also."
  • A stranger could understand what the player does.
  • It fits in 3 minutes of playtime.
  • You can build it with what you learned Days 1–2: move, collide, pick up, score, timers.

Read One-Sentence Pitch for more examples and what to avoid.

#Get instructor sign-off (10:35–10:40)

Show your written pitch to your instructor. They'll ask two questions:

  1. "Can you build this with two days of Godot?"
  2. "What's the smallest version of this that's still fun?"

Answer both. If approved, the instructor signs your index card. You cannot start Godot until the pitch is approved. If you get a "not yet," ask: "What should we cut?"

#Paper Prototype + Cross-Team Playtest

#Build the paper prototype (10:40–11:00)

On blank paper (no Godot yet), draw your game:

  1. Draw the play area — what does the screen look like? Where do things start?
  2. Draw the player (as a shape) and any enemies, obstacles, or collectibles.
  3. Write down the rules in plain English on a separate piece of paper:
    • What does the player control?
    • What kills the player / ends the run?
    • How do you win (or is survival the goal)?
  4. Make a simple "turn sequence" — if you were playing this as a board game, what happens each round?
💡Use index cards as "game pieces"

Cut index cards into pieces for your player, enemies, and pickups. Physically move them around. It sounds silly — it works.

See Paper Prototyping for what a good prototype looks like.

#Cross-team playtest (11:00–11:20)

Swap your paper prototype with the team next to you. You are not allowed to explain it — hand it over and watch.

The playing team:

  1. Read the rules sheet. (1 min)
  2. Play through the game using the paper pieces. (10 min)
  3. Answer these questions on a sticky note for the building team:
    • "The moment I understood the game was when ___."
    • "I got confused when ___."
    • "One thing I'd add/change: ___."

The building team sits silently and watches. Write down what you observe — where did they hesitate? What did they misunderstand? That's your user research.

#☕ Break

Read your feedback. Talk to your team. Decide if anything in your design needs to change based on what you saw.

#Create the project (11:35–11:45)

  1. The Engineer creates a new Godot project. Use your game's name as the folder name (no spaces — use underscores: firefly_game).
  2. Before anyone writes code: decide how you'll share files. Read Sharing Files on a Team and pick one method now. Write it on your index card next to your roles.
  3. Set up your project window size (Project Settings → Display → Window) — 480×720 is a safe default, but you can choose your own if your game calls for it.
  4. Create your first scene: Scene → New Scene → add a plain Node2D as root → rename it Main → save as main.tscn.

#Get the player moving (11:45–12:00)

  1. Inside your Main scene, add a child CharacterBody2D → rename it Player.
  2. Add these children to Player:
    • AnimatedSprite2D (or Sprite2D if you have a still image)
    • CollisionShape2D → New RectangleShape2D or CapsuleShape2D
  3. Attach a script to Player. Here is a basic movement starting point:
extends CharacterBody2D

@export var speed = 300

func _physics_process(delta):
	var direction = Vector2.ZERO
	if Input.is_action_pressed("move_right"):
		direction.x += 1
	if Input.is_action_pressed("move_left"):
		direction.x -= 1
	if Input.is_action_pressed("move_up"):
		direction.y -= 1
	if Input.is_action_pressed("move_down"):
		direction.y += 1

	velocity = direction.normalized() * speed
	move_and_slide()
  1. Press F6 (Run Current Scene) — your player shape should move with arrow keys.
💡CharacterBody2D vs Area2D

We used Area2D in Dodge the Creeps because the player was just detecting hits. For a game where the player needs to push against walls or gravity, CharacterBody2D + move_and_slide() is better. See Movement and Input for both patterns.

Checkpoint: ✅ Approved pitch on index card · ✅ Paper prototype played by another team · ✅ Player moves in Godot.

#Open Studio

Keep building! Most teams will use Open Studio to continue progress toward MVP. Your first goal: get your first obstacle, enemy, or collectible into the scene.

#First addition options (pick based on your paper prototype)

Whatever your game needs first, these three patterns cover most tiny games. Each assumes your player is the CharacterBody2D named Player you just set up. If your player needs to detect hits or pickups, add a small Area2D + CollisionShape2D as a child of Player first (name it Hitbox) — that gives you body_entered/area_entered signals for free, the same reason Day 1's player used Area2D.

Obstacle that ends the run — a new Area2D scene (with Sprite2D + CollisionShape2D) that emits a custom signal when body_entered fires on the Player, connected to a game_over()-style function in your main script. You built this exact pattern twice already: Day 1's Player hit signal, Day 2's pickup. Full code and wiring in Area2D.

Collectible that gives points — same Area2D scene structure, but the signal adds to your score and the node queue_free()s itself. Identical to Day 2's pickup — the code is in Area2D, and hooking it to a score label is in Score and UI.

A simple enemy that moves on its own — a CharacterBody2D that patrols back and forth, flipping direction with is_on_wall(). Full scene setup and script in Movement and Input → A simple enemy that moves on its own (patrol).

See Spawning and Timers if you want any of these spawning on a loop instead of placed by hand — that's Day 1's MobTimer pattern.
